# Bunkering Explained

*A practical introduction to the global marine fuel industry*

## 1. Why bunkering matters

Bunkering is the process of supplying fuel to ships. Although it appears to be a relatively small part of maritime operations, it is one of the fundamental processes that keeps global trade moving.

Around **88% of global trade** is transported by sea, representing roughly **USD 20 trillion worth of goods annually**. Without fuel, vessels simply cannot operate.

Every year approximately **250 million metric tons** of bunker fuel are supplied worldwide (an IMO estimate), representing a market worth roughly **USD 175 billion**, although both volume and market value fluctuate significantly with oil prices.

Fuel costs are also one of the largest operating expenses for shipping companies:

* Cruise ships: approximately 10% of operating costs
* Tankers and container vessels: up to 60% of operating costs

Because of these economics, improving bunker operations can have an enormous financial impact.

---

# 2. What exactly is bunker fuel?

The shipping industry primarily operates on three fuel categories.

## Fuel Oil (Residual Fuel)

The traditional heavy fuel used by large commercial vessels.

Common variants include:

* High Sulphur Fuel Oil (HSFO)
* Very Low Sulphur Fuel Oil (VLSFO)
* Ultra Low Sulphur Fuel Oil (ULSFO)

Large ocean-going vessels generally use fuel oil for propulsion because of its relatively low cost.

---

## Gas Oil / Marine Diesel (Distilled Fuel)

Cleaner and lighter fuels used for:

* auxiliary engines
* generators
* smaller vessels
* vessels unable to burn heavy fuel

Examples include:

* Marine Gas Oil (MGO)
* Marine Diesel Oil (MDO)

---

## Alternative Fuels

Growing rapidly due to environmental regulations.

Main alternatives include:

* LNG (Liquefied Natural Gas)
* Methanol
* Ammonia
* Ethanol (limited)

The expectation expressed during the session is that LNG currently has the strongest momentum because:

* roughly half of new vessel tonnage on order is LNG-capable
* technology is mature
* global infrastructure is expanding

Many alternative-fuel vessels are actually **dual-fuel**, allowing them to operate on LNG and conventional marine gas oil depending on availability.

---

# 3. How ships use different fuels

Most large vessels carry more than one fuel.

A typical vessel contains:

* one large tank containing fuel oil for propulsion
* one smaller tank containing gas oil for generators and auxiliary systems

Alternative-fuel vessels usually also retain conventional fuel capability because LNG or methanol is not available everywhere.

---

# 4. How bunkers are delivered

Fuel reaches ships in three ways.

## 1. Bunker barges (≈90%)

This is by far the dominant delivery method.

A bunker barge comes alongside the receiving vessel and pumps fuel directly into its tanks.

Depending on the location, these may be:

* river barges
* ocean-going bunker tankers
* dumb barges pushed by tugboats

---

## 2. Truck

Mostly used for:

* small vessels
* ports with limited infrastructure
* inland shipping

---

## 3. Pipeline

Common at fixed terminals but much less flexible than barges.

---

# 5. Why barges dominate

Moving the fuel is usually much easier than moving the ship.

Instead of forcing a container vessel worth hundreds of millions of dollars to sail elsewhere for fuel, a bunker barge simply comes alongside.

This allows bunkering while the vessel is:

* loading cargo
* unloading cargo
* waiting at anchor
* undergoing crew changes

This is known as **SIMOPS (Simultaneous Operations)**.

---

# 6. Where bunkering happens

Contrary to popular belief, bunkering is not limited to ports.

Fuel can be supplied:

### At berth

While cargo operations are taking place.

Very common for:

* container vessels
* RoRo vessels
* cruise ships

---

### At anchorage

Ships waiting offshore receive fuel while:

* awaiting berth
* awaiting orders
* awaiting cargo
* waiting for market conditions

---

### Offshore / Off Port Limits (OPL)

Ships can also bunker outside port limits.

Reasons include:

* avoiding deviation from trading routes
* avoiding port fees
* avoiding customs
* operational flexibility

Unfortunately, offshore bunkering is also commonly used for sanctioned trades and other opaque activities.

---

# 7. Major bunkering hubs

The world's largest bunkering locations include:

* Singapore
* Rotterdam / ARA
* Fujairah
* Houston
* Panama
* Cape Town
* Suez region
* Hong Kong
* Tokyo Bay

Singapore is by far the largest bunker port globally.

Approximately **54 million tonnes** of bunker fuel are supplied there annually—around one-fifth to one-quarter of the global market.

---

# 8. The bunker supply chain

Unlike many industries, bunker supply involves a surprisingly large number of participants.

A single delivery may involve:

* Fuel producer
* Trader
* Broker
* Physical supplier
* Bunker barge owner
* Charterer
* Barge operator
* Vessel crew
* Receiving vessel crew
* Port authority
* Shipping agent
* Surveyor
* Laboratory
* Classification society

This complexity explains why transparency has historically been difficult.

---

# 9. Major players

The industry consists of several groups.

### Physical suppliers

Companies owning or operating bunker vessels.

Examples include:

* Monjasa
* Peninsula

---

### Traders

Companies buying and selling fuel without necessarily owning delivery assets.

Examples include:

* Bunker Holding
* Trafigura
* Vitol

---

### Oil majors

Companies such as Shell, BP and others have returned to bunkering after previously reducing their involvement, largely because new regulations and alternative fuels require greater capital investment.

---

# 10. Why the market lacks transparency

One recurring theme throughout the discussion is that bunker data is surprisingly difficult to obtain.

Reasons include:

* many privately owned operators
* multiple legal entities
* complex charter structures
* poor reporting
* limited public statistics
* commercial incentives to keep information private

Unlike container shipping, there is no definitive global register of bunker vessels or bunker deliveries.

---

# 11. Fraud has historically been a major issue

The session describes bunkering as an industry that has traditionally suffered from:

* quantity disputes
* quality disputes
* measurement inaccuracies
* opaque trading

Historically fuel quantities were determined using manual tank measurements ("ullaging").

Because fuel volume changes with:

* temperature
* vessel trim
* vessel list
* pumping speed

there was considerable room for manipulation.

---

# 12. Mass Flow Meters (MFM)

One of the industry's biggest technological improvements has been the introduction of **Mass Flow Meters**.

Instead of estimating tank volume, they directly measure the mass of fuel flowing through the pipeline.

Benefits include:

* more accurate quantity measurement
* reduced fraud
* digital records
* improved confidence

Singapore made MFMs mandatory in 2017.

Rotterdam and other ports have begun adopting similar regulations.

---

### Limitations

A Mass Flow Meter only measures:

> "How much fuel passed through the meter."

It does **not** prove:

* where the fuel went
* whether the correct ship received it
* whether bypass pipes were used

Surveyors therefore still play an important role.

---

# 13. Electronic Bunker Delivery Notes (EBDN)

Singapore introduced mandatory **Electronic Bunker Delivery Notes** in 2025.

An EBDN records:

* supplier
* receiver
* fuel type
* quantity
* location
* timestamp

This creates a complete digital audit trail.

Several other ports are expected to follow over the coming years.

---

# 14. Quality management

Fuel quality is another important aspect.

Several versions of the "truth" may exist simultaneously:

* nominated quality
* stated quality
* certificate of quality
* laboratory results

All of these may differ.

Fuel quality is generally assessed against **ISO 8217**, the international marine fuel specification.

---

# 15. Commercial contracts

Most bunker contracts are based on:

* BIMCO bunker terms
* supplier-specific terms
* customer-specific terms

The contract also specifies:

* fuel grade
* delivery conditions
* payment terms
* quality requirements

Payment terms vary from:

* cash in advance
* cash on delivery
* up to 90 days credit

---

# 16. Pricing

Prices are usually fixed when the deal is agreed.

Participants often hedge fuel price exposure using financial instruments.

Because a typical bunker delivery is approximately:

* **600 tonnes**
* around **USD 600 per tonne**

an average bunker delivery represents approximately **USD 360,000** worth of fuel.

---

# 17. Disputes

Disputes generally fall into three categories:

### Quantity claims

"Did we receive the amount we paid for?"

---

### Quality claims

"Did the supplied fuel meet specification?"

---

### Time or delay claims

"Did delays during bunkering cause commercial losses?"

These disputes may result in:

* commercial settlements
* discounts on future deliveries
* demurrage claims
* legal proceedings

---

# 18. Why Singapore leads the industry

Singapore has become the benchmark because it combines:

* mandatory Mass Flow Meters
* mandatory EBDNs
* licensed bunker operators
* licensed bunker vessels
* strong port authority oversight

This combination creates significantly greater transparency than most other ports.

---

# 19. Future direction

The session concludes that the bunker industry is gradually becoming more transparent, driven by:

* digitalisation
* electronic documentation
* regulatory pressure
* alternative fuels
* improved operational data

However, transparency remains uneven. While many established players see value in greater efficiency and trust, parts of the market still benefit from opacity and resist change.

---

# Key Takeaways

* Bunkering is a **USD 175+ billion industry** supplying roughly **250 million tonnes of marine fuel annually**.
* Fuel represents up to **60% of vessel operating costs**, making bunkering strategically important.
* Most bunkers are delivered by **barges**, often during cargo operations or at anchorage.
* The market is **highly fragmented and historically opaque**, involving many intermediaries and limited public data.
* **Singapore** is the global leader in bunker digitalisation through **Mass Flow Meters** and **Electronic Bunker Delivery Notes (EBDNs)**.
* The industry is transitioning toward **greater transparency, digital documentation, and alternative fuels**, with LNG currently leading the energy transition.
